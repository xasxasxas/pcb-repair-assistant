PCB Repair Assistant

A repair-bench assistant for electronics technicians. Photograph a faulty circuit board, add a short note, and it reads the board's asset tag, finds the components, diagnoses the fault and recommends whether the board is worth repairing.

Web demo: Streamlit · Offline version: Android tablet app (same models) · Final submission: Mon 30 Nov 2026

What happens at the bench

A board arrives for repair. The technician works through six steps on one screen. Each step is labelled as AI (pretrained deep learning) or ML (classical models we trained ourselves).

  1. AIRead the asset tag. OCR finds the yellow tag in the photo, crops and cleans it, and reads the board family, revision and serial number.
  2. AIFind the components. An object detector boxes every part it recognises and counts them by type.
  3. Look up the board's history. The serial number pulls age and previous repairs from the board registry. This is a database lookup, not AI.
  4. Describe the fault. The technician types a short note, e.g. "Green deposits on the pins, salt residue across the lower edge."
  5. MLDiagnose. A text classifier turns the note into one of six fault categories and shows the words that drove the decision.
  6. MLRecommend. A Random Forest predicts the risk that the board is beyond economic repair (BER) and the expected repair hours. The job card is saved to a log that can be exported as CSV.

The AI and ML techniques, one by one

All scores are on held-out data the models never saw during training.

AI

OCR · Tesseract

pretrained, used as-is

Tesseract reads text with an LSTM neural network. Raw OCR on a whole board photo fails, because silkscreen text and part markings drown out the tag. Our pipeline first finds the yellow tag by colour, crops it, boosts contrast and applies an Otsu threshold, then runs OCR on that clean crop. A parser fixes common OCR confusions, such as O read as 0.

Serials read correctly on the 50 demo photos: raw 15/50 → with tag cleanup 49/50

AI

Part detector · YOLOv8

COCO-pretrained, fine-tuned by us

YOLO is a convolutional network that predicts boxes and part types for the whole image in one pass. We fine-tuned it on 137 real board photos from an open dataset (Roboflow 100, CC BY 4.0), merged into 7 part types: IC, capacitor, resistor, connector, transistor/diode, inductor and switch. The boards are split so that no board appears in both training and testing.

Test mAP@50 0.35. Strong on ICs (0.63), switches and connectors; weak on tiny resistors and capacitors, which this week's work targets.

ML

Fault classifier · TF-IDF + Logistic Regression

trained by us

TF-IDF turns each note into a vector of word and word-pair weights, scored higher when a term is frequent in that note but rare overall. We compared Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression and Linear SVM with 5-fold cross-validation. Logistic Regression was chosen because it gives probabilities and per-word explanations: each word's weight for the predicted class shows why the model decided.

Test accuracy 95% on 120 held-out notes · CV F1 0.977

ML

Repair predictor · Random Forest

trained by us

Inputs are board type, age, previous repairs and the predicted fault. A Random Forest classifier, a vote of many decision trees, gives the BER risk; a Random Forest regressor gives the repair hours. Feature importance shows what drives each decision: board age and fault type drive scrap decisions, and fault type drives repair time.

BER ROC AUC 0.82 · repair-hours error 0.43 h (guessing the average: 1.24 h)

archived

Speech-to-text · Whisper

kept in code, off in the web demo

The voice-note input is switched off for the classroom web demo, since the hosted app can't rely on a microphone, and the technician types the note instead. The code is kept and can be switched back on. The offline Android app keeps its own on-device speech recognition (Vosk).

Data: the repair records (600) and board histories are synthetic, generated from fault-specific phrase banks with a fixed seed, so no real facility data is used. The board photos are real and openly licensed.

This week: finding the small parts

The problem. Board photos are about 2,000–2,900 px wide, but the detector looks at the whole photo shrunk to 1,024 px. A small SMD resistor ends up only a few pixels wide, too small for the network to see, which is why resistors and capacitors scored only about 0.25.

The fix: tiled (sliced) detection. Enlarge the photo, cut it into overlapping 1,024 px tiles, and run the detector on each tile, so small parts keep enough pixels. Then map every box back onto the original photo and merge the duplicates.

Measured, not assumed. We scored both modes against the hand-labelled parts on the 22 held-out test boards (4,709 parts), at full photo resolution:

Reading the result. Without any retraining, tiling finds about twice as many parts and raises recall from 34% to 46%. The cost is about four times the compute, still under a second on a laptop CPU, and somewhat lower precision. The model was trained on shrunk photos, so it has never seen parts at the enlarged size. Retraining on tiles fixes that mismatch, which is the next step. Inductors and switches (under 20 test parts each) are left out of the mean because a handful of boxes swings their score.

The app now has a "Detailed detection (tiles)" switch, so the class can see the difference live, and the Model Evaluation tab shows this table.

Known limitations

Repair records are synthetic. The detector still misses some of the smallest parts on dense boards, and it sometimes boxes the asset tag as a connector. Rare part types (inductors, switches) have too few examples to score reliably. The web demo takes typed notes only.
